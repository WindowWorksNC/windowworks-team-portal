# Andersen series overview

Andersen is our number one brand. This is the map of their lines and where each
one fits. Every other Andersen module assumes you know this.

> **Fibrex** is Andersen's own composite, made from wood fiber and polymer. It
> doesn't rot, doesn't need painting, and is 2.5 times stronger than vinyl,
> which lets them build a slimmer frame and more glass. It comes up throughout
> the Andersen line, and the 100 Series gliding door module covers it properly.

## The series

**E-Series.** Architectural collection. Aluminum clad over a wood frame, so no
rot on the exterior. The most versatile line they make.

E-Series is being discontinued. A new 500 Series replaces it, carrying many of
the same features but without some of the heavier customization options. The
transition is expected to start around October 2026, though what Andersen has
told us so far is limited. If a customer mentions the 500 Series, that's what
they mean.

**A-Series.** Fiberglass clad, Fibrex framing, real fiberglass sashes. Built
for coastal and high wind load. Excellent, low volume in our market.

**Big doors.** Moving glass wall systems: bifold, lift and slide, multi-glide.
They need serious wall engineering, minimum 2x6, and won't work on 2x4. That
suits new construction, not replacement. We have sold them to restaurants and
homeowners.

**400 Series.** Andersen's Best category, and our workhorse. Wood interior with
a vinyl clad exterior, mid-price, proven over decades. More on it below.

**200 Series.** Also vinyl clad. Windows use nominal sizing that can't be
customized, so mostly new construction. But the 200 Series sliding glass door
is a top seller and a great product.

**100 Series.** Fibrex composite, no wood at all. Newer, evolving, and we sell
a lot of it. The new 100 Series double hung should become one of our top
sellers over the next decade. Their gliding windows are already our go-to for
screened porch conversions.

### More about the 400 Series

It comes in two windows that share the same frame and differ in materials. The
**Tilt Wash** is vinyl clad wood outside, wood inside. The **Woodwright** is the
same but adds Fibrex to the exterior, which is why it's the upgrade. Because they
share the frame and most specs, moving a customer between them is easy.

Both use traditional wood window sizes, so no custom sizing on the double hungs.
Three interior colors, seven exterior.

## Telling whether it's already an Andersen

Plenty of customers will tell you they have Andersen windows, and believe it, and
be wrong. Sometimes it's what a previous owner said, sometimes it's what the
builder claimed, sometimes it's just that Andersen is the name people know.
Always check for yourself.

From inside the house, look at the lower right corner of the glass. If it's an
Andersen you'll see the **AW logo etched into the glass.** Etched, not a decal,
so it can't be washed or peeled off. On a double hung both sashes carry it.

Andersen has been etching glass since at least September 1962, so this covers
almost anything you'll walk into.

Older than that and it's harder. They sometimes stamped the company name into
hardware, so check the lock. Material helps too, mostly by ruling Andersen out:
**an all vinyl window is never an Andersen.** They have only ever made vinyl clad
wood, which is vinyl outside and wood inside. Vinyl on the inside as well means
it isn't theirs.

[Do I Have Andersen Windows?](https://www.windowworksnc.com/do-i-have-andersen-windows/)
has the full version, and the quiz covers this.

## What we lead with

The full guidance is in The lineup in week 2, but the three most common cases
are worth knowing now.

- **Full frame replacement of standard old wood windows** is the 400 Series
  Tilt Wash
- **The same job on a colonial style house** is the 400 Series Woodwright
- **Insert or replacement windows** are the 100 Series, double hung, casement,
  and so on

## Andersen Aluminum, which we don't sell

**Andersen Aluminum.** Sold only in Arizona, California, New Mexico, Nevada, and
Texas, and built for those climates. Not available here.

## The colors match across every line

**Andersen white is the same white on every series, every style, and every
cladding material.** Same for black, dark bronze, Sandtone, and Terratone.

So you can mix aluminum clad, vinyl clad, and Fibrex in one house and the colors
still match. That's a real selling point when somebody is combining E-Series with
400, or adding 100 Series gliders to a porch.

## How Andersen ranks their own lines

Andersen sorts the series into four tiers, and customers who have been on their
site will have seen this language.

- **Premium.** E-Series and A-Series
- **Best.** 400 Series
- **Better.** 200 Series
- **Good.** 100 Series

Good doesn't mean cheap and it isn't a warning. The 100 Series product statement
is "the smartest alternative to vinyl," and it's the one we sell most of.

**Also worth reading:**
[An In-Depth Comparison of the Different Andersen Window Series](https://www.windowworksnc.com/an-in-depth-comparison-of-the-different-andersen-window-series/).
Colors, hardware finishes, and shapes for every line, and the quiz covers this
one too.

## Why we sell them

Andersen was founded in 1903 and is still family and employee owned rather than
publicly traded. Their customer service is genuinely good, and they stock parts
for windows they stopped making in the 1960s.

Hans Andersen ran a family lumber company in Bayport, Minnesota, bought his
brothers out, and started making windows. Shipping assembled windows was hard,
so he sold frames knocked down for assembly on site and boxed the sashes
separately, then shipped nationally by rail. He also built it as a mass
production line.

They invented the vinyl window in the 1960s, decided it wasn't structurally
sound, and abandoned it. Today they make no all-vinyl window.

The strongest thing you can say is that we have them in our own homes.

# 100 Series single and double hung

The 100 Series is Andersen's entry level line and an extremely popular one. It's
all Fibrex fiberglass composite, with no wood anywhere, inside or out.

There are two hung windows in the line and they're very different products.

## The single hung

Only the bottom sash operates. The top is fixed. It doesn't tilt in.

It's a side load, meaning you pull clips and pop the sash out, and that is
designed for service and sash replacement rather than cleaning. It isn't a
simple job.

There's noticeable play in the sash when you move it, which reads worse than the
window actually is. That's a design characteristic rather than a quality problem,
and it's why this has never been a favorite of ours.

## The double hung

Recently released and the opposite story. Both sashes operate, it tilts in for
cleaning, there's no play in the sash, and the frame is solid.

**This is the one to sell.** We expect it to become one of our top sellers over
the next decade, and it's our answer whenever someone is shopping vinyl
replacement, especially if they want black on the outside.

The field it competes in is Renewal by Andersen, Marvin Infinity, Pella, and
ProVia, all of which sit at the higher end of fiberglass.

## What Fibrex gives you

It doesn't rot. It's made from recycled sawdust and wood bound with polymer resin
from fiberglass, so it's a genuinely green product, and it's **2.5 times stronger
than vinyl.**

The color is integral to the material rather than painted on top. Scratch a
painted vinyl window and you find a different color underneath. Scratch this and
you have to go a long way in before you reach anything different.

## Sizing and options

It uses **nominal sizing**, which the [Sizing conventions](module:REF-030) sheet
explains. That suits replacing existing vinyl from new construction. It also
customizes in eighth inch increments, so it's forgiving and cost effective.

**Exterior colors** are white, Sandtone, Terratone, dark bronze, and black, and
two tone is available.

**Grilles** differ between the two, which is easy to get wrong because they sit in
the same module. The **single hung** offers all five Andersen configurations:
Finelight between the glass, Finelight with exterior grilles, SDL, FDL, and FDL
with an energy spacer. The **double hung** offers Finelight between the glass and
nothing else. Removable interior isn't available anywhere in the 100 Series. The
[window anatomy](module:REF-090) sheet has the cross-sections.

**Hardware** is always the metal slimline lock, which the
[Never sell, always include](module:REF-040) sheet covers.

# 100 Series casement and awning

Casements and awnings are both crank operated, so they're covered together here.

**Casements** crank out to the right or the left. **Awnings** crank upward, and
only about 30 degrees rather than 90, which is worth saying out loud to a
homeowner who's picturing full ventilation.

Either one can be ordered stationary, meaning it looks the same but doesn't open.

**A stationary unit can never provide egress,** and it looks identical to an
operable one from outside. The [Egress and sleeping rooms](module:REF-060) sheet
covers what to check before you put one in a room somebody sleeps in.

## Stationary casement or awning, versus a picture window

If you have several units in a row and some don't open, a stationary casement
or awning keeps the visible sash line so the whole row looks uniform from
outside. Nobody can tell which ones operate.

A picture window has no sash. The frame runs across in one piece.

Picture is less expensive, and above certain sizes casement isn't available so
picture is the only option. Below that, it's a look decision.

## The casement conversion

This is a real sales play. A customer with a bank of old wood double hungs can
go back with 100 Series casements as inserts and completely change the look of
the house at a reasonable price.

The 100 Series is the line for this, because its eighth inch custom sizing is
cheap and forgiving. On the 400 Series the same conversion costs considerably
more.

## Details

These share the Fibrex, the glass packages, and the colors with the rest of the
100 Series.

**Screens** come in fiberglass mesh, which is the less expensive option, or
TruScene. A casement only ever takes a full screen.

**Locks** differ between the two. An awning gets one on each side. A casement
gets a single lock in the lower corner, with folding hardware.

# 100 Series gliding windows

**If you're selling a gliding window at Window Works, this is almost certainly
the one.** Across every brand and every line we carry, it's the one we reach for.

One panel slides and the other is fixed, and you choose the operating side
**viewed from the exterior.** Unlike a double casement, you can't open both.

## Why it wins

Fibrex lets it go bigger than vinyl. A double reaches six by six, and triples run
to roughly twelve feet.

Triples come symmetrical, or uneven with a fixed picture center and gliding
ends.

**Screened porch conversions are where we sell the most of them.** Those
openings are whatever someone framed years ago, with no standard sizing, and
this window customizes to fit. Pair it with 100 Series casements and picture
windows to fill a whole run.

The real advantage on a porch is space. Casements crank out into wherever people
are walking or sitting. Gliders don't take any space at all, so a patio, a set
of stairs, or a table and chairs stays usable.

Also works as a pass through from a kitchen to a porch or patio, at a fraction
of what a multi glide or bifold would cost.

Available without a nailing flange, so it can go in as an insert where a
picture window was, or where two double hungs come out and the divider between
them goes with them.

## Easy Breeze

The competitor you'll meet on porch conversions. Local contractors and
dealers push it hard in the Triangle.

It's a vinyl triple track system, and the panel is vinyl rather than glass. It's
plastic, and you can press on it with your hand.

For a four season room where the customer wants real heating and cooling
performance, it isn't a comparable product.

## Hardware

Always the slimline metal lock, at about five to ten dollars a window. We don't
present it as a choice, we just include it, because the plastic one breaks.

# 100 Series gliding door

This is our budget gliding patio door, and a good one.

It's Fibrex, so it doesn't rot, and it's priced right around vinyl and sometimes
under ProVia. Well below the 200, 400, E and A Series.

## Fibrex versus vinyl, and this is where it matters most

**Fibrex is 2.5 times stronger than vinyl.** If you're going to make that
comparison anywhere, make it here.

Vinyl gliding doors get problematic over time. The glass panels are heavy and
plastic framing struggles to carry that weight, so you get warping and
operation problems as the panel presses into the frame. Fibrex carries it.

Be fair about the exception: ProVia puts steel reinforcing in their door
panels, which helps a great deal. Not all vinyl is equal, and saying so makes
you more credible, not less.

The cheap gliding doors at a big box store are a different matter. That is
where the real problems come from.

## What it is and isn't

**Two panel only.** There's no four panel option in the 100 Series.

One panel operates and the other is fixed. **Choose the operating side viewed
from the exterior.**

**It can't be ordered knockdown.** The frame is integrally built and doesn't screw
together in the field, which is the opposite of the 400 Series.

## Details

**Sizes** start at about five feet and run up to eight feet tall, with six feet
being standard, and they customize.

**Hardware is limited to two handle sets,** both metal. Tulsa comes in white,
Sandtone, or black. Afton covers satin nickel, bright brass, antique brass, and
black.

**The auxiliary foot lock is always included**, at about fifteen dollars. It's a
slightly different design from the 400 Series version but does the same job.

**Screens** are fiberglass mesh only, on a standard sliding screen panel.
TruScene isn't available on any door because it's considered a hazard.

Glass options are the same as the rest of the line.

# 100 Series picture and specialty

Picture windows are most of what you'll sell out of this part of the line.
Specialty shapes are available too, but limited.

## The frame rule, and it applies to the whole 100 Series

**The frame is 3-1/4 inches per side.** That means 6-1/2 inches of any unit
dimension is frame, in both directions.

A 12 by 12 picture window leaves you 5-1/2 inches of glass. It doesn't look
good.

We've had this go wrong. A customer was told, went ahead anyway, saw the window at
pickup and wanted to return it. Once it's ordered, we own it and so do they.

So on anything small, a walk in closet or a short wide unit, look at a specialty
window instead. The rule holds across the gliders, the single hung and the
casements too, and the smaller the unit, the more pronounced the frame.

## Where picture windows work

Big sizes are where they're excellent. They run up to six by eight because Fibrex
carries the weight, and down to one by one, customizable in eighth inch
increments.

**Screened porch conversions**, where they fill openings alongside the gliding
windows.

**Living rooms** where a customer has a bank of old wood double hungs and tells
you they never open them anyway. Converting those to energy efficient picture
windows is a nicer look, and we've done plenty of them.

**Sleeping rooms need an egress check first.** A picture window is fixed, so its
clear opening is zero and it can never satisfy egress on its own. That doesn't
rule it out, but it does mean something else in the room has to provide the way
out.

The [Egress and sleeping rooms](module:REF-060) sheet has the rules, the North
Carolina numbers, and the three ways to give a customer the look anyway.

## What it looks like

There's no sash, so the frame runs across in one piece. Compare that with a
stationary casement or awning, which keeps the sash line. In a row of units, that
difference shows.

No welded sash corner either, so you get a clean miter with none of the rough
edges you see on a vinyl window.

## Specialty shapes

Trapezoids, half circles, and so on are available in the 100 Series, but the
thick frame works against them. Small sizes lose most of their glass, and
lining up angles for a roofline is harder with a heavy profile.

For most architectural shapes, Andersen specialty or E-Series is the better
answer. The 100 Series is less expensive, but more limited, and it won't look as
good.

# 400 Series Tilt Wash double hung

One of two double hungs in the 400 Series. The other is the Woodwright, and
they're different windows. **Always say Tilt Wash or Woodwright, never "400
Series double hung."** The shared name confuses customers, contractors, and
everyone else.

This is Andersen's workhorse, sitting above their lower lines and below the upper
ones. It's one of our top sellers as a full frame application, and it's easy to
service because it comes apart and back together cleanly.

## Construction

It's a wood frame with a wood interior in unfinished pine, vinyl clad on both the
frame and the sashes. The sill is Fibrex composite with a 14 degree pitch, which
is the standard pitch for old wood windows.

## Why it sells

**Stock sizes match traditional wood window sizes.** Full frame tear out to the
studs and no customization needed.

That keeps the continuity of the home's look, limits siding and exterior work,
and if you go back with the same size moulding inside you usually avoid paint
lines. Not guaranteed, and it isn't in our labor contract, but usually.

Both sashes tilt in, though it's harder than on the upper lines because there's no tilt latch built into the lock.

## Config traps

**Custom sizing exists in eighth inch increments and will price you out of the
deal.** On an old wood window you almost certainly don't need it.

**Black or dark bronze interiors are only available matching the same
exterior.** Black outside allows black or white inside. Dark bronze allows dark
bronze or white. White outside with black inside isn't available on this
window, though Andersen allows it on other lines.

**There's no factory stain.** It comes as unfinished pine, and staining it
afterwards is somebody else's job. We don't offer staining.

## Never sell

**Optional lift hardware.** Andersen moved framing up for energy performance
and it no longer lines up, sitting above the glass. They still sell it. We sold
expensive bright brass hand lifts to a customer who chose the window for them
and then couldn't fit them. Fine on the Woodwright, never here.

## Details

**Hardware** comes two ways, both metal. The standard locking keeper is white,
stone, or black. The Estate locking keeper covers antique brass, bright brass,
distressed bronze, and satin nickel, and it comes up most in kitchens where
somebody is matching cabinet hardware.

**Exterior colors** run to seven. White is 75 to 80 percent of what you'll sell
and black is second. Terratone and forest green show up in wooded and rural
settings.

**Trim** from Andersen is Fibrex, and it's nice when it works, but the spacing
fails in brick openings. We use PVC brickmould instead, and white PVC matches
Andersen white closely enough that most people don't paint it.

**The product ID** is `TW` followed by the sash size, so `TW2636` is 2 feet 6 by
3 feet 6.

# 400 Series Woodwright double hung

This is the other 400 Series double hung, and it's a step above the Tilt Wash.

## Why they share a name

When both launched the frame was essentially the same: same PVC wrap on the
upper frame, same Fibrex sill, both sized to replace old wood windows. They
have diverged since, and the sashes were always different.

Which is why you never say "400 Series double hung" to anybody.

## What makes it the upgrade

**The sash is architecturally correct.** Go into a colonial home, or anywhere in
New England, and the old wood sashes look like this one.

**There's more wood in it.** The jamb liners are wood where the Tilt Wash uses
PVC, which is where the name comes from.

**The sash exterior is all Fibrex** rather than vinyl clad, which gives it a more
beveled, cleaner profile.

**The tilt release is easy.** The mechanism is built into the lock, so you unlock
it, keep turning, and the lower sash disengages. The top sash has tilt pins.

**You get wood species options,** which are unfinished pine, maple, or oak.
There's still no factory stain.

## Same as the Tilt Wash

It uses the same sash sizing, so the same full frame pitch applies: tear out to
the studs, no customizing needed, the look stays continuous, and siding and
interior work are limited. Paint lines still aren't guaranteed.

Exterior colors, glass options, and trim limitations are all the same too, so
it's PVC brickmould again.

## Different from the Tilt Wash

**Hand lifts work fine here**, so sell them if a customer wants them. This is the
opposite of the Tilt Wash rule.

**There's no estate hardware**, which was discontinued long ago. Easy tilt release
is the only style, though you can still pick finishes. If a customer has old
Woodwright windows and wants to match the original hardware, that can be special
quoted.

## Product ID

The product ID is `WDH` plus the sash size, so `WDH3442` is 3 feet 4 by 4 feet 2.
Unit dimensions run larger because of the frame.

One measuring note: stated height runs from the top of the frame to the very
bottom of the sloped sill, so the interior sits a little higher than the number
suggests.

# 400 Series casement

There's one casement in the 400 Series. Unlike the double hungs, there are no
variants to keep straight.

It cranks out to the right or the left, and it can be ordered stationary, which
comes up on a three panel where the center doesn't open.

**A stationary casement can never provide egress,** and it looks identical to an
operable one from outside. The [Egress and sleeping rooms](module:REF-060) sheet
covers what to check before you put one in a room somebody sleeps in.

## Construction

It's vinyl clad over a pine frame, wrapped 360 degrees including the sash, with the
wood sealed inside the vinyl. So it doesn't rot, and it has the strength and
rigidity of real wood behind it.

Painted interiors use a baked on polyurethane rubber base paint, so there are no
brushstrokes. That holds across the painted Andersen line.

## Finishing the inside

**A casement jamb is 2-7/8" deep,** so it never reaches the inside face of the
wall. Tilt Wash and Woodwright are 4-9/16" and reach flush on a 2x4, which is why
this comes up on casements far more often.

The remaining depth gets finished with either extension jambs or sheetrock
returns. Those look completely different and one costs a lot less, so it's worth
raising at the appointment. The
[Wall depth and interior finish](module:REF-070) sheet covers both, with the
standard depths.

## Sizing

Casements don't match old wood double hung openings, so a full frame conversion
almost always means customizing. Stock sizing does exist and is worth checking
anyway, because occasionally you get lucky.

Custom costs more, the same as it does on the double hungs.

## The kitchen sink play

Even in a house full of double hungs, people often want one casement behind the
sink. Cranking beats reaching across the counter to lift a sash.

Pair it with estate hardware matching their cabinet finishes and you have a
specific, high value recommendation you can make in almost every kitchen.

**Check where the crank lands before you order.** A casement cranks from one side,
and if that side is the faucet side, the crank and the faucet can end up in the
same place. We've had them line up exactly, which meant the window couldn't go in
and had to be reordered.

So look at the sink, look at the faucet, and pick the operating side that puts the
crank clear of it.

## Details

**Grilles** come up less often here. It's one sheet of glass and most people want
the view.

**Exterior colors** are the same seven as the rest of the 400. White leads, but
black is climbing on casements, particularly on contemporary homes or a house
doing casements throughout.

**Traditional versus contemporary** differs only in the interior glass stops. It's
hard to see on the website and clearer in the quoting schematic.

**The product ID** starts with `C`. The numbers after it are Andersen's own
designation with no sash or nominal logic behind them, so there's nothing to
decode.

Andersen makes a very wide range of shapes: ovals, circles, trapezoids, arches,

On anything angled, follow the roof line. That isn't a matter of taste, it just

# 400 Series patio doors, hinged and gliding

The 400 Series has a hinged patio door and a gliding one, and they're covered
together here.

## Andersen undersizes doors

**Their 6'8" is 79.5". Doors in the field are around 82".**

For a standard 6'8" opening you need the **6'11" option, 82-3/8"**.

It's a measuring convention. Other door companies quote the panel and add the
frame separately. Andersen gives you the whole product including the frame, so
selecting 6'8" because the existing door is a 6'8" gets you something that won't
fit.

## Hinged, the French wood door

It comes double or single, full lite, with a traditional profile and more wood
around the glass.

**The multi-point lock is the best in the industry.** Deadbolt locks the entire
handle set so you can't lift or push the handle. And Andersen builds the
active and passive panel activation into the hardware, so there's no astragal
with flip levers to fumble with. Push either handle and both panels operate.

Worth contrasting with Therma-Tru, whose multi-point we won't sell because
it fails. Andersen builds in house. Therma-Tru ships to distributors who
assemble. Same feature, opposite verdict, and that contrast explains our whole
brand position.

The 400 Series hinged door is frozen in time. The A-Series looks nearly
identical with far more options. This one still works well, but colors and
options are limited.

**The hinges adjust,** so you can raise, lower, or shift the panel. That matters
here because our clay soil never stops moving houses, so a door that goes out of
reveal can be brought back rather than replaced.

## Gliding, the French wood glider

This one has traditional thick lower panels on a ball bearing roller track.

**Raised track, almost like a monorail,** so dirt and debris fall away instead
of jamming the rollers the way recessed tracks used to.

**The rollers adjust with a Phillips head** from the inside, for the same reason
the hinges do.

200 and 400 Series gliders share the same frame, rollers, and hardware. Only
the panel profile differs.

Standard width is 71.25", and gliders go much larger than hinged doors because the
support system is different. They run up to what Andersen calls 8 foot, which is
an actual unit of 95-1/4", and 8 foot in height.

The handle is a C handle, which is standard across most brands.

## Two rules

**Order knockdown, always.** Safer to handle, less damage, one person can
deliver it, and there's a small price break. Our crews assemble in 15 to 20
minutes. We don't offer assembled. Note the 100 Series glider can't be
ordered this way.

**Include the auxiliary foot lock, always, without asking.** Thirty to
thirty five dollars. A real deadbolt shoot bolt operated with your foot, and it
can be drilled for a second position so the door locks while vented.

## Hardware finishes are style specific

There isn't a universal palette, so the finish depends on which style you pick.

- **Albany** is white, black, or stone only
- **Tribeca** is white or stone
- **Anvers** gets satin nickel, bright brass, or ORB
- **FSB** is stainless only
- **Ashley Norton** has the widest range

The names correspond across hinged and gliding, and every style and every finish
is a different price.

## Blinds between the glass

These are available on stock sizes and lost if you customize. They're excellent
for kids and pets because they can't be damaged, and they never need cleaning
because they're sealed in.

## Pro tip that applies everywhere

The website and the quoting software disagree, and neither is reliably current.
**Go with what Vendo will let you build.**

## Product IDs

`FWG` is the French wood glider. `FWI` is in swing hinged and `FWO` is out swing.
Out swing is discontinued but still shows on the website.

# DSA doors and the Breezeport

DSA is the Door Store of America, a Raleigh company building real wood doors. We

In practice the Breezeport is what we sell most of, and there is a specific reason

[why you do not want a Dutch door](https://www.windowworksnc.com/why-you-dont-want-a-dutch-door-and-what-to-get-instead/)

about wood doors can go. We started getting calls from other states asking us to

## What makes it worth showing

{{video: https://www.loom.com/share/5539458e1f92419f8dcc8550f35b7f58}}

The door in that video is Jeff's front door, filmed at his house.

Two things sell it.

**It vents without letting anything in.** A knob in the face of the door opens a
venting window, and there is screen mesh behind the grille. So the door can be
open to the breeze with the panel still closed and locked, and without a storm
door in front of it. Nobody else builds one like this, which is why the calls
come from out of state.

**It has presence.** DSA builds to varying heights. Jeff's is an 8 foot door, and
that scale is most of why it reads the way it does from the curb and from the
foyer. They also make a standard height, and the Breezeport can be built as a
double door. His is also built to the thicker panel, so it is heavy. Justin makes
the point in the video that a door that heavy has to be hung exactly right, and
that a correctly hung one stays wherever you leave it rather than drifting.

## The wood and the glass

DSA builds in mahogany, knotty alder, and Accoya, and we can quote any of them.

Mahogany is the one that comes up most. It handles Carolina heat, humidity, and
temperature swings without the swelling and shrinking some species go through.
Most door mahogany is red mahogany. DSA uses white mahogany, which costs less and
does the same job, and once the door is stained the difference is not visible.

Decorative and privacy glass options run across the line. The door in the video
has reed glass, a privacy glass that passes light without a view through it. Arch
tops and double doors are both available.

The
[five reasons to love DSA master crafted doors](https://www.windowworksnc.com/five-reasons-to-love-dsa-master-crafted-doors/)
post is the one to send a homeowner who wants to read more.

## Where wood sits against fiberglass

Wood costs more than fiberglass, every time, and it is likelier to break down. The
warranty is the clearest way to see it. **DSA covers the door for one year** from the
date they ship it, against lifetime coverage on a fiberglass slab. The insulated
glass carries ten years. The warranties sheet has the comparison across every brand
we sell.

That is not a reason to avoid the conversation. It is the reason the DSA
conversation starts with the overhang and the finish rather than the styles. A
homeowner who wants a real wood door and has the porch for it gets a door nothing
else matches. A homeowner who wants the look without the maintenance wants
fiberglass.

## Thickness, and what it means for hardware

A standard exterior door panel is 1-3/4" thick, and most DSA doors are that. Some
of their doors can be built to 2-1/4" instead, which is what the door in the video
is.

When you spec the thicker panel, the handle set has to be rated for that depth.
Check it rather than carrying over the hardware from a 1-3/4" quote.

## The overhang rule

**We do not sell a DSA door where we know the warranty will be voided.** A
homeowner will tell you they are fine with it. DSA covers the door for one year even
when everything is done right, so a voided warranty leaves them with nothing from
the day it ships. There is no later point at which the coverage was going to help
them.

DSA's warranty excludes "exposure to conditions exceeding published guidelines."
The guideline is a ratio, stated in the warranty and again in the claim form.

- **Overhang height** is measured from the bottom of the door to the bottom of the
  overhang
- **Overhang length** is measured from the outer end of the overhang back to where
  it meets the house
- **Length has to be at least half the height**

A door under an overhang 10 feet up needs at least 5 feet of overhang. A deep porch
passes easily. A shallow eave over a tall entry does not.

Measure it at the appointment, before anybody falls in love with a door.

The ratio applies to mahogany and knotty alder, and DSA also wants minimal direct
sunlight and shielding from the elements on those two. Accoya is warranted without
an overhang, and we still advise against it on a short overhang for the same reason:
a one year wood warranty is a one year wood warranty. A short overhang points to
fiberglass.

## Finish, and what else voids coverage

Any door not ordered factory finished by DSA has to be stained and sealed with
three coats of UV top coat on all six sides within 30 days. The finish, sealant,
and caulk have to come from DSA or an authorized dealer. A finish somebody else
applied is not covered, and neither is damage from an inappropriate solvent,
cleaner, or brick wash. DSA recommends a fresh clear coat yearly after that.

Four more exclusions worth knowing before you quote:

- **Black paint or black stain on mahogany or knotty alder** is not covered. Black
  is fine on Accoya, though DSA does not recommend light or transparent stains
  there
- **A glass storm door voids the warranty**, because it traps heat against the wood
- **Prehung units only**, in a residential home, bought through a verified DSA
  dealer
- **Non-sanctioned modifications** to the door are not covered

DSA also recommends a multipoint lock on doors taller than 80", to keep a tall panel
from warping.

## Filing a claim

Claims go through the dealer, which means us, and DSA requires a dealer inspection
before a claim opens. Their form asks for the sales order and PO numbers, the
species, style, size, glass, swing, whether the unit has a multipoint lock, the
overhang measurements, and the finishing history. It also asks for a set of photos:
a wide view from the curb, the full overhang from several angles, the door from
outside and from inside, a wide view of the foyer, and detail shots of the damage.

Take the overhang photos at the sales appointment rather than after a claim. A file
with the overhang documented from the start is a file that does not stall.
